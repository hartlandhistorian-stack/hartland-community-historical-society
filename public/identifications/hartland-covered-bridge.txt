	•	This postcard, issued in 1899, shows piers in the river upon close inspection. The original piers were constructed from wood in a grid pattern and filled with rocks gathered from local farmers’ fields. During major renovations between 1920 and 1922, the wooden piers were replaced.
	•	The building, initially known as the Riverside Inn and managed by M. F. Thornton, later became Clark’s Inn, complete with livery stables behind it. David Clark, son of Allison, was known for offering carriage rides from the CPR to Clark’s Hotel, always distinguished by his top hat and pipe. In 1920, the hotel and livery stable were put up for sale, including all the furniture. The property featured five sleeping rooms, an office, a ladies’ parlor, and a large dining room. In the background is the uncovered bridge, opened in 1901 as a toll bridge. Note the privy located on the bridge between the first and second spans, just to the left of the pole. (Photo courtesy of Ralph Day)
	•	On July 4, 1901, Premier Tweedie and his colleagues arrived by train in the mid-afternoon and were greeted by an impressive procession. Leading the way was S. S. Miller, Parade Marshall, on horseback, followed by a brass band, decorated bicycles, and a carriage carrying the Directors of the Hartland Bridge Company. Other buckboard wagons transported Members of Parliament and representatives from the Foresters and the Women’s Christian Temperance Union. A large crowd of citizens joined the parade as it moved from the train station through the festively decorated town, across the new bridge to Victoria Corner, and back to a platform on the bridge’s east side, where the official opening ceremony was held. (Archives P61-442)

	•	This rare photograph was discovered hidden behind another picture in an antique frame purchased at a second-hand shop in Nova Scotia by Greg Britton, who grew up in Hartland. He originally bought the frame but was delighted to find an image capturing the opening day of the Hartland Bridge, taken from the Somerville side. The photograph is high-resolution, and when zoomed in, the vibrant activities of July 4, 1901, are clearly visible, as marked on the back. (Photo courtesy of Greg Britton)

	•	On the afternoon of July 4, 1901, Hartland was filled with excited citizens eager to witness history: the official opening of the Hartland toll bridge. Dr. Curtis chaired the ceremony, which began with a welcome address to the Premier, distinguished guests, and local citizens. The program featured several speeches, including a humorous one by Premier Tweedie, and mentioned the hopeful prospect of the bridge becoming toll-free in the future. The celebrations continued with a foot race and a ticket draw for a kerosene table lamp with a colorful shade, won by Charles Rideout. (Photo courtesy of Buddy Barss)
	•	Festivities continued on the green beside the new bridge during its official opening on July 4, 1901. The photograph captures a motorized swing, along with races and games in progress. If you look closely, you can spot decorative flags and the greenery adorning the bridge. (Photo courtesy of Ralph Day)
	•	This is one of the stereoscope pictures, which uses two slightly different angles to create a sense of natural depth when viewed. The photo was taken by Charles McCormack, President of the Hartland Bridge Company and Superintendent of bridge construction, after the bridge was completed. New photo enhancement technology has revealed a sign on the bridge that reads: “Tool Bridge Forty Dollar Fine for Driving Your Animals on this Bridge Faster Than a Walk.” (Photo courtesy of Charles McCormack’s family)
	•	This zoomed-in photo from Buddy Barss’s collection showcases the toll booth on the east end of the uncovered bridge. Right from the start, residents grumbled about the tolls and doubted they would last. Many locals found ways to avoid paying, such as using an ice bridge during winter. In response, the bridge directors lowered prices, stopped charging pedestrians and funeral processions, and only collected tolls during certain hours. The fees were meant to cover maintenance and pay the toll gatekeeper, but harsh weather rapidly deteriorated the wooden structure, and toll revenue proved inadequate. Tolls continued until 1906, when a government election promised to eliminate them and assume responsibility for maintenance. There was no fanfare or official announcement; one day, the toll gatekeeper, James Pearson, simply didn’t show up for work. (Photo courtesy of  Buddy Barss)
	•	This photo of the uncovered Hartland Bridge and the Town of Hartland was taken between 1908 and 1920. To the right, a small structure built by the Polchies family served as their summer retreat. The Polchies family became well-known in the community, often trading or bartering baskets, snowshoes, and fish with local residents.
A well-loved story recalls a time when the Chief visited the Polchies' family and fell ill during the visit. Dr. Lorne MacIntosh was called to treat him, and afterwards, presented the Polchies with a bill. “Oh, that’s a lot of money; too much,” he protested. The doctor replied, “It’s not what you do, it’s what you know.”
Later that fall, when Dr. MacIntosh needed his snowshoes repaired, he took them to Polchies. When Polchies presented his bill, the doctor exclaimed, “Oh, that’s a lot of money to fix these shoes!” Polchies responded with a smile, “It’s not what you know, it’s what you can do.”

	•	World War I soldiers march off the bridge, accompanied by a little boy hurrying alongside them. An early group departed in 1914; this photograph remains unidentified.

	•	On April 5, long-awaited renovations to the bridge were finally completed after years of weather damage and strain from log drivers had left it in disrepair. The repairs were much needed, as the wooden structure was weak and overdue for an overhaul. However, just as the bridge was ready to reopen, disaster struck. A night of heavy rain caused water levels to rise and ice to break up, a perfect storm. Normally, ice would flow down the eastern shore, but on April 6, 1920, it swept unexpectedly down the western side, destroying two spans and a pier. Once again, the town faced a devastating setback. (Photo courtesy of Hazel Palmer)
A vivid account from a newspaper clipping dated April 22, 1920, captures the drama of that day. Kenneth Cox, who would later serve in World War II, was on his way with his father to cut wood for Arthur Baird when the bridge gave way. They lived on the west side of the river and had just reached the bridge approach when Henry Ginson, returning home after delivering milk, raced his horse off the shaking bridge as ice gnawed away at the pier below. Frank “Long” Shaw, running toward the western end, shouted to the Coxes, "Stay off, stay off! She's going to go any minute!" And she did. As “Long” Shaw set foot on solid ground, the bridge collapsed behind him, timbers tumbling into the river, and in moments, Hartland’s vital link was lost. (Henry Ginson was Ward Ginson’s father)

	•	The disaster of April 6, 1920, forced residents to rely once again on ferries, ice bridges, or long trips to Florenceville or Woodstock to cross the river. Frustrated, citizens called for a steel bridge, but the cost was prohibitive. Instead, the government launched a major project: replacing the wooden piers with concrete, rebuilding the two lost spans, and covering the entire bridge to improve its durability. Though people initially disliked the covered bridge, describing it as a long, dark tunnel and worrying about young couples lingering or unsavoury characters hiding inside, they ultimately accepted it out of necessity. After two years of construction, the bridge reopened in early 1922. Little did anyone realize how beloved this iconic covered bridge would become for future generations. (Photo courtesy of Vanessa Dickinson)

	•	An unidentified woman stands cheerfully on the newly constructed narrow boardwalk along the western span, which was being replaced. Behind her, the ice bridge is visible, marked by sleigh tracks left by locals who used it each winter to cross the frozen river. Small fir trees lined the track, guiding travellers safely across during snowstorms. (Photo courtesy of Inez Roy)

	•	By the beginning of 1922, the bridge was fully covered, as seen here from the east side of the river in Hartland. Ben Irving, a local carpenter, crafted the bridge’s first sign, which had not yet been installed. The new tunnel-like structure quickly drew complaints from residents: the sound of horses’ hooves on loose floorboards echoed through the valley, disturbing sleep, and the darkness inside was unsettling. Maintenance crews had to return several times to add spikes to the floor, and eventually installed lights to address these issues.

	•	In 1944, the Department of Transportation built a new concrete approach with reinforced supports on the east end of the bridge. They also installed lights at that time, improving safety and visibility. In March 1945, construction began on a covered pedestrian sidewalk along the south side, effectively creating a separate attached walkway that paralleled the main bridge. Pictured on the right are Henry and Lois Downey, Clayton Cox's parents. (Courtesy of Jason Parsons and Ralph Seeley)

	•	Over the years, the bridge's fronts have been painted in various colours—white, bright red, and natural weathered barnboard. Locals were never shy about sharing their preferences: the bold red was short-lived, the white was accepted, but most favoured the natural, weathered look. When Doris Wilkinson Tompkins was about 13 or 14, she took this photo between 1948 and 1950. A local tradition held that, upon entering the bridge, you should close your eyes, hold your breath, and make a wish, believing it would come true by the time you reached the other side. My fun-loving father embraced this ritual when we entered the bridge, and he knew his children were in the backseat following the tradition. Let’s just say he never received a fine because his speed dropped to below a casual walk. (Courtesy of Jim Leal)
 
	•	To mark the Town of Hartland’s 100th anniversary of incorporation in 1918, a truly unforgettable event was held: a white tablecloth, four-course dinner on the bridge, followed by a live jazz band, street dancing, and a spectacular fireworks display. This vision became a reality thanks to dedicated volunteers and a shared sense of community pride. The 2018 celebration remains a cherished memory, fondly remembered by all who attended.

	•	Charles A. McCormack held the dual roles of President and Superintendent of Construction for the Hartland Bridge Company. Born around 1841 to William McCormack and Eunice DeGrasse, Charles spent much of his youth with his uncle, Edward DeGrasse. Although his formal education ended at fourteen, he became skilled in trapping, lumbering, and carpentry, eventually establishing himself as a successful farmer. Among his many accomplishments were the construction of several highway bridges—including the Aker Creek Bridge at Lower Brighton—and the design of Hartland’s first water system. On May 30, 1901, Charles experienced a rare moment of professional pride: as superintendent, he wrote a long-anticipated letter to himself, in his capacity as President, confirming the bridge’s completion. He then forwarded this letter to the provincial government, officially marking the successful end of a truly remarkable project.

	•	A ferry, located a mile south of Hartland, connected the east side of the river to Victoria Corner on the west, once an important stagecoach stop well before Hartland had its name. When the eastern spans and pier of the bridge were lost in 1920, ferry service resumed at this historic crossing, a mile below Hartland, where the steady current made it ideal for a cable ferry. The approaches on both banks could easily be made passable for teams and automobiles. Construction of a new ferry boat began in Hartland in May 1920, with the cable soon to arrive. The ferry operated daily from 6 a.m. to 10 p.m. The image here shows the rustic ferry crossing the St. John River to Upper Victoria Corner. Arthur Hovey operated the ferry; the woman in the photo remains unidentified.
