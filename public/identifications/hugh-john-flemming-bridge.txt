	•	In 1958, the beginning of the construction of the Hugh John Flemming Bridge began with the east abutment. Workers unidentified. (Pronicial Archives P55-1544)

	•	Spruce timber gridwork was used to build the arches. Floyd Page stated, “50-60 foot limbed spruce timbers with the bark still on, were steam hammered down into the pan. Dennis Clendenning was responsible for running the crane, putting the steam hammer on, and pounding all day, never shutting it off, for two shifts. Sandy Hawkes made the steam using an air-cooled, flash-burning iron-horse engine fueled by stove oil. It made steam in 1.5 minutes and sat on a drag and piped steam through a hose to the hammer.”

	•	Ed Montgomery recalled, "When construction started, my dad, Bob Montgomery, saw an opportunity. With hundreds of workers and no food or drink available, he purchased a trailer, cut a large square hole in its side to install a hinge, and transformed it into a canteen. He sold pop, chocolate bars, Hatfield chips (naturally), homemade sandwiches, and a crowd favorite—pickled eggs. When I was just 11 or 12, I took over running the canteen whenever Dad was away. I often wondered if his earlier experience running the canteen inspired him, in 1967, to purchase Jimmy Miller's store and transform it into The Bluejay.” (Photo courtesy of Patricia Paget)

	•	August 30, 1959, Beverley and Bill Hayward stand on the eastern deck to see the progress. To the left of the west end, the white building was the cement plant, and the next level down was the woodworking building. The causeways were on both sides of the piers. 	•	Looking east. After cement was poured on the arches, workers climbed the staging and crib work to keep it moist throughout the winter. Floyd Page, age 25, with the help of Angus Shaw, kept the heat burners running around the clock. 

For every 24-hour period, either Gene Smith or Ray Tedlie worked as timekeeper in a small building. Workers would arrive to collect a round, penny-shaped tag with their number on it, along with any equipment needed; both the tag and the equipment were returned at the end of each shift.

	•	Support pillars were built to support the deck.

	•	The bridge approaches consist of two slab spans on the west end and three on the east bank, one of which crosses the CPR railroad track and the existing highway. The highway features a quarter-cloverleaf pattern linking Hwy 105 on the east and a semi-cloverleaf junction with Hwy 103. A unique aspect of construction was that suitable gravel, which allowed the concrete to withstand 4,000 lbs. per square inch after 28 days, was sourced directly from the bridge site—a rare occurrence. Another innovation was the use of polyethene 'cocoons' with space-heated interiors during winter construction. (Provincial Archives P155-1545)

	•	Spanning 1,144 feet from bank to bank, the Hartland Bridge features a 26-foot roadway supported by seven spandrel-type reinforced concrete arches. It was the first bridge in the Atlantic provinces to use this spandrel construction system. Concrete pillars of varying lengths support the deck, which carries the roadway and 24-inch sidewalks on each side.

	•	South of the two west piers, the road goes up the hill past the woodworking building and storage sheds to the cement plant. The causeway used during construction is in the foreground; it was washed out three times in two years. On the west pier base, workers built a cofferdam, some reaching 60 feet below the riverbed. After removing the water from the cofferdams, they installed steel beams and rods to prepare for concrete pouring. (Provincial Archives P155-1549)

	•	At 12:30 pm, dignitaries gathered for a luncheon at the White Swan Inn in Somerville. The official ribbon cutting took place at 2:30 pm on June 4, 1960, drawing a crowd of up to 3,000 people. The celebration featured two canteens and colorful decorations, welcoming the 30 cars in the official party. After the ceremony, the procession of cars crossed the new bridge and continued through the covered bridge in a parade.

	•	1958: Steel was stored on the north side of the west abutment. Looking west, you can see the beginnings of at least five cofferdams and the bases of the concrete piers along the causeway. On the first tier above the river to the right are the woodworking buildings, where crews built wooden forms and barrels for the concrete. On the second tier above the river is the cement plant. Sand was hauled from the north island, and rocks were gathered from the riverbed. (MC-1396-5-43 HJF)
