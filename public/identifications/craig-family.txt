Woodie Craigs
Woodford (Woodie) Craig left school early in grade 10, having little interest in academics. His father soon put him to work on the family farm on Rockland Road. Although farming was not his passion, Woodie found satisfaction in working with machinery and dismantling equipment. He attended Moncton Vocational School and later, during an apprenticeship in Montreal, further developed his skills as a machinist and completed courses in electrical and acetylene welding. Woodie married in September 1942, and in 1946, he and his wife returned from Montreal. Following the town’s approval for Washington Craig, Woodie’s father, to operate a 35-by-70-foot service station and machine repair shop on Orser Street, next to the town hall, they expanded their business to sell Cockshutt farm equipment.

Washington and Woodie Craig initially operated a car dealership and custom fabrication, while Woodie also practiced his trade as a machinist. When his father returned to farming, Woodie was left to manage the business on his own. Sensing an opportunity, he promptly sold the dealership and redirected his efforts toward his true expertise, metalworking. Despite pressure to change course and his family's initial doubts, Woodie crafted several sample bolts, donned a suit, and drove to Fredericton to meet Premier Hugh John Flemming in person. His bold move proved successful; the Premier referred him to several interested businesses. Woodie left Fredericton that day with a contract in hand.

Woodford Craig stands in his machine repair shop and service station, founded in 1946. Trained as a machinist, Woodie began by making parts and bushings for local customers. His philosophy was that no project was too big.
He soon expanded his focus to make attachments for snow plows, wings on trucks, graders and loaders. The name changed from Craig’s Service Garage to Craig’s Machine Shop.



  The Craigs built and installed the first wing on a wheel loader. The accompanying photo highlights Woodie Craig’s original V-plow and wing plow designs. By 1952, he had secured contracts and was shipping a diverse range of products to Atlantic Canada, Quebec, and Ontario. And now, the business started to take off.

In 1955, Woodie proudly stands next to the first loader bucket, prepared for delivery to Frank G. Hough.
There was a willingness to create. In 1960, Woodford Craig designed and built a car ferry to cross the river at Keswick.
 



There was a willingness to take on any challenge.  In 1960, Woodford Craig successfully bid on building a car ferry to cross the river at Keswick. This was Woodie’s most memorable project because he was not sure that it would float! It did and carried passengers and cars across the river for many years. 

Woodie proudly stands with his only son, John, who joined the company in 1976 and became President in 1985. The photo was taken at the 30th Anniversary celebration. Their partnership brought new ideas and a strong community spirit to the business. By 1979, they had 63 employees and specialized in building custom snow plows, machinery trailers, logging equipment, pavement rollers, and land-clearing gear. In 1977, they built a 10-ton snow plow for J.D. Irving’s 880B Clark skidder to clear woods roads. They also made a V model 630.11 plow for the Grand Canyon and shipped heavy-duty clearing tools, like root plows and blade rakes, to Honduras and a customized v plow and snow wing to Egypt. Most of their products were custom-made for each client. By then, the workshop had grown to 22,500 square feet and included modern machines like an electronic flame cutter and an ironworker punch. The flame cutter was so precise that it could cut a paper doll from steel faster than scissors could cut paper.

This late 1970s photo captures the Craig Manufacturing team standing inside a giant wood chip bucket, symbolizing their ambition and the impressive scale of their operations. From the beginning, Craigs supported the local economy by sourcing supplies from nearby businesses. In 1965, the company’s core team consisted of 24 employees, with six carpenters joining during expansions. Their ingenuity and craftsmanship soon earned recognition across Canada. As demand increased, the business expanded, resulting in a broader product line and continual growth. By this time, Craigs was producing heavy equipment such as buckets, pusher blades, shovels, plows, and gravel buckets. One notable accomplishment was a massive 6,600-pound bucket—three tons of steel—built in 1964 for a Hough payloader tractor powered by a 700-horsepower engine. Each year, the team manufactured approximately thirty snow plows to meet customer needs.

Claude Ginson captured this photo of the entire Craigs Manufacturing crew, proudly posing with a selection of their products to showcase at a trade show in Ottawa, Canada in 1977. The response by the dealers there was amazing.

Woodie Craig was known for his warmth and genuine interest in people. He was a familiar face at Nicholson Hardware, often chatting with customers and friends. Woodie valued personal connections and travelled by car, train, or plane to meet clients and build lasting relationships. In his office, he used tools like an intercom and telephone, and kept a CB radio in his Lincoln, along with a walkie-talkie to communicate with employees. He and his son began each day at the plant by 7 a.m., believing an early start gave them an edge. Without government support, Woodie—and later his son John—sold products across North America, from Alaska and the Northwest Territories to as far south as British Honduras. By 1962, Woodie had expanded the service station to 50 by 90 feet, purchased the old town hall in 1964, and constructed two new buildings in 1965 to improve efficiency. The photo was taken in 1989, after Craig's Machine Shop expanded and became Craig's Manufacturing.

This photo, taken on August 17, 1988, showcases the 13,000-square-foot expansion at Craig Manufacturing, which was built for the production of snow plows and other equipment. Although it would have been easier to locate the facility outside of town, the Craigs chose to keep it local reflecting their deep roots in the community and commitment to family values. From the beginning, the Craigs have cared deeply for their employees. “I have a terrific group of employees, and without them, Craigs would not be where it is today,” said Woodford Craig at the opening of the new addition, where he also gave a heartfelt tribute to his family. The family's loyalty to the community continues with John and Ben, who have supported numerous local initiatives.

Craigs Manufacturing expanded its Orser Street location in 1988 and again in 2001, adding state-of-the-art equipment, including a computer numerical control lathe and an electronic cutter. In May 1992, to make room for further growth, Craigs demolished the original John T. G. Carr home, a building that had been relocated from Main Street to Orser Street in the early 1900s and had served as a rental property for many years. After World War II, Lloyd Nicholson purchased the house for use as a hardware store, and it later became the first site of the Dr. Walter Chestnut Library. When the library moved to the historic Post Office, Craigs became the main benefactor, helping to create a finished art gallery and a children's reading room. 


This photo, taken on June 22, 1992, features three generations of the Craig family: John Craig, his son Ben Craig, and his father, Woodford Craig. Together, their innovative leadership has driven the growth of Craig Manufacturing, a trusted name in custom heavy construction equipment with six offices across Canada and the USA. The Craigs are deeply committed to their community, providing financial support for projects such as the Dr. Walter Chestnut Library and the New Brunswick Day Covered Bridge Events. They have donated equipment, established a scholarship at Hartland High School, and contributed to the new Hartland Community School, helping to fund another gym, a larger cafetorium, and expanded facilities. Over the years, they have also supported the Covered Bridge Golf and Country Club and, more recently, the new Day & Ross Community Centre. Their public spirit is evident not only in their generosity but also in their service. Both John and Woodie served on Town Council, while John’s wife, Betty Lou (Wilson) Craig, broke new ground as Hartland’s first female mayor. John and Woodie also served on the Carleton Redevelopment Committee after the 1980 fire. John has supported the Hartland Fire Department, served on the NB Road Builders, coached baseball, and was the first Chairman of the Carleton Chamber of Commerce. Betty Lou has contributed tirelessly as well, directing school musical plays, helping organize the 100th Anniversary of the Hartland Bridge, and founding the library and the Joy Kindergarten at the Baptist Church.


John Craig, President of Craig Manufacturing, is pictured with the company’s signature yellow bucket prior to the major fire in 2005.


On the evening of May 7, 2005, a devastating fire reduced the expanded 41,000-square-foot Craig Manufacturing facility to rubble. After 59 years of hard work and dedication, the loss was crushing for owner John Craig, his family, and the entire community. The fire left many multigenerational workers suddenly unemployed, a heavy burden for John. Amid the chaos, a team managed to rescue the server containing vital drawings, accounting records, and human resource files, all essential for restarting the business. Despite the devastation, the decision to rebuild was made. The Craig spirit and the Craig team of employees worked tirelessly to get back to work. The silver lining was that it gave an opportunity to streamline the production process.

After the fire, Craig Manufacturing rebuilt their business in the Hartland Industrial Park. They welcomed manufacturing employees on December 12, 2005, and office staff on December 23, 2005. The official opening of the new 71,000-square-foot site took place on May 6, 2006, just 364 days after the disaster and celebrated the 60th Anniversary at the same time. Achieving this rapid recovery and streamlining the production process required exceptional organization, many sleepless nights and the exceptional cooperation of all of the trades people working on the building.  


The family business welcomed a new generation when John’s son, Ben Craig, joined after the 2006 Hartland fire. A University of Toronto graduate, Ben contributed to the company’s growth as Craigs expanded with a new branch in Cambridge, Ontario in 2008, followed by another in Red Deer, Alberta in 2013. In 2018 their newest branch was in Tennessee, USA to serve new customers and expand production in the USA market To support this growth, they hired four dedicated salespeople from abroad and installed advanced steel-cutting tables at the plant. Ben became President in 2014. The photo shows Ben and his proud father, John Craig, celebrating Craig Manufacturing’s 75th anniversary in 2021.

Woodford C. Craig, father of Washington Craig and grandfather of Woodford Craig, purchased a 1909 McLaughlin Buick, serial number 1722, registered in 1910 with license plate 273. Known as the first car in Hartland, was also tagged as the devil in early times of scaring horses, has been carefully maintained in mint condition by generations of Craigs, renowned for their mechanical skills. Woodford Craig Sr. ran a lumber operation and was known for his colourful character and sharp wit, a family trait. His true passion was fishing, and as an outfitter. He loved spending time with people. He and his wife, Rebecca, established a camp along the Miramichi River, which still stands today. The Buick remained in the family: Washington owned it until 1972, then Woodie until 1995, and now John. The car was always a highlight in Hartland parades. While it still appears in parades today, it is rarely driven and now rides atop a trailer for special occasions.
