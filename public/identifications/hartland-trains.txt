	•	Collision at Maple Street Crossing, July 7, 1905: On a Monday morning in Hartland, the up freight train hauled by Engine No. 170 was standing in the yard when it was struck head-on by the southbound Plaster Rock train. The crash left both engines almost fit for the scrap heap. Passengers on the Plaster Rock train were badly shaken. A. J. McLean of Bristol suffered some bruises. Burke of Johnville and his daughter, en route to Debec, were thrown from their seats by the sudden jolt. The young woman was thrown against the stove, loosening several teeth and dislocating her wrist. Dr. Curtis tended to her injuries. Superintendent Newcomb, with a wrecking crew, cleared the track within a few hours. An investigation was launched to determine responsibility for the accident. Local officials reportedly settled with those who suffered damages. 
	•	In 1914, citizens of Hartland gathered at the train station to bid farewell to the 67th Regiment and Brighton Engineers as they departed for World War I. The troops left Woodstock for training in Quebec before heading to the front lines. At the decorated Hartland station, two carloads of soldiers were greeted by waving flags and the sound of a band playing “God Save the King” as the train arrived. When it departed, few eyes remained dry, knowing that many would not return.
	•	The first contingent passed through Hartland on August 20, 1914. The Brighton Engineers, led by Colonel Melville, distinguished themselves in battle by being the first to use a pontoon bridge to cross a river under German fire.

	•	In 1928, Frank Hagerman’s Model T collided with a train at the Maple Street crossing. He walked away unharmed. Well known in the community, Hagerman was determined to improve railway safety. By December, a new signal alarm was installed at Curtis Crossing, marking a lasting change inspired by his experience.
	•	Clearing the tracks, 1940s. Since 1874, the train has been vital to the valley, providing passenger service, connecting the region, supporting timber and agricultural trade, spurring economic growth, and creating a new transcontinental market. Each day, two trains passed through Hartland—one northbound and one southbound. (Photo courtesy of Don Oliver)
	•	A track washout caused a derailment at 1 a.m. in 1952 at Acker’s Creek, resulting in the deaths of two Aroostook Junction CPR railmen, the engineer and the brakeman, when a seventeen-car northbound freight train carrying fertilizer and gasoline plunged into the river. The water was high and swift at the freshet level. Ten cars derailed; eight were partially or fully submerged, and the locomotive was completely underwater.
	•	The Hartland Train Station once featured five sets of tracks, with warehouses lining the outer rails for easy freight loading. Keeping the tracks clear during winter required many hands. In November 1963, north and southbound passenger rail service, along with express and mail service, came to an end. Trains had once replaced stagecoaches and horses; now trucks and buses had taken over from the passenger train.
	•	On Tuesday, May 13, 1958, the Honourable Vincent Massey, Governor General of Canada, and his wife arrived in Hartland in the afternoon. Massey declared Wednesday afternoon a school holiday, and local children gathered at the train station to celebrate. On behalf of the Queen, he inspected the Boy Scouts, Girl Guides, Canadian Legion, I.O.D.E., and Women’s Auxiliary.
	•	The train tracks needed regular inspection and repairs, as the weather often damaged the rail bed. Photo taken in 1963.
	•	Standing alone since 1874, it holds a hidden history within its bones. Silent, it waits for something lost in time. The platform, worn smooth by boots, hurried footsteps, and steel trolleys of suitcases, now collects dirt and weathers gently with time. Empty benches seem tired from years of waiting. You can almost hear the faint whistle, the murmurs of voices, the shuffling of parcels and bags, and the laughter and excitement of children awaiting a returning soldier. The tracks, long since pulled from their bed, stretch in both directions like unfinished thoughts. The faded sign still clinging to its name declares that this place once mattered. What remains feels heavy is the lingering presence, waiting for one more train that will never come.
	•	In 1995, the train tracks were removed, revealing the Craig Manufacturing property in the background.
	•	In the early 1950s, Hatfield Industries loaded boxes of potato chips into boxcars. Owner Heber Hatfield was known for his remarkable memory. He would stroll along the line of railway cars beside his office, memorizing each car number. Back in his office, he would recite the numbers in exact order to his secretary. Some numbers were as long as ten digits, and there were often more than a dozen cars to remember. (Photo courtesy of Barb Nicholson Layte)
