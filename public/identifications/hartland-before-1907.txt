	•	This photograph, taken on April 14, 1885, looks upriver toward the Becaguimic from the ice-jammed shore of the Saint John River. Despite the poor resolution, you can make out both the steel train bridge and the wooden highway bridge spanning the Becaguimic.
	•	This photograph of Hartland, taken between 1891 and 1895, looks north along the street. The first building on the left was the C. Humphrey Taylor Store, followed by John Barnett's residence. The third building was the Keith & Plummer General Store, established before 1897, and the fourth was John T. G. Carr's first store, opened in 1884. The eighth building in the row was the Burtt Store and Hall, while the ninth housed the Matheson Blacksmith Shop and Hall. On the right side, the large home belonged to John T. G. Carr, which was later replaced by a three-storey brick building and became the Day & Ross Headquarters. The fenced area eventually became the site of the C. Humphrey Taylor Block, and the partially visible home on the right belonged to George Boyer. Built in 1860, Boyer’s home was the Village of Hartland’s first post office and drug store.
	•	The Commercial Hotel, located east of the railroad tracks, was partially burned in 1912. An article in the Observer from March 1, 1911, noted: “George G. McCollom, the energetic proprietor of the Commercial Hotel, has installed a new bathroom, one of the finest in Carleton County. In addition, Mr. McCollom had the office floor oiled, giving the place an up-to-date appearance. Commercial travellers who recently visited the hotel commented favourably on its improved look. They all joined in wishing Mr. McCollom success.”
	•	This photograph, taken from Carr’s Hill, showcases the uncovered bridge and its toll booth, offers a partial glimpse of Main Street shops, including the distinctive cupola atop C. Humphrey Taylor’s store and, in the foreground, highlights the prominent Commercial Hotel situated across from the railroad tracks. (Courtesy of  Buddy Barss)
	•	The David H. Keswick General Store (Originally George Burtt’s Store & Hall) was established in 1887 on upper Main Street. Over the years, it was transformed into the Neighbourhood Theatre and later into Stedman's 5 & 10. In its final years, the building served as the location for Russel Smith’s Alumico building supplies before it burned down in 1968. David Keswick (1871–1933) married Ena McAdam (1875–1935), and together they had one child, Alice (Keswick) Jewett (1911–2003). In addition to running the store, Keswick also operated a lumber business.
	•	Estey and Curtis Drug Store began in a small shop inside the Taylor Block at King (later Orser) Street and Main Street, Hartland. Samuel Miller founded it in 1895, naming the store after Dr. Isaac Curtis and Dr. Alfred Estey. In 1897, Miller bought the Hartland Drug Store’s inventory and expanded the business to offer a wide range of medicines and sundries at low prices. He later partnered with Frank A. Aiton. The shop quickly outgrew its original space, so on August 2, 1911, Miller bought a larger brick building from John T. G. Carr. (Current headquarters of Day & Ross in 2026)
	•	The Exchange Hotel was built in 1866 with a livery stable and served travellers arriving by steamboat and train. In 1908, Worden Frank Thornton bought the hotel from D. T. Day, who kept the livery business. The hotel had 20 guest rooms, an office, a writing room, a parlor, and a dining room for 40 people. In 1919, Thornton added big front windows, opened a fruit and candy store, and made the dining room into an ice cream parlor. G. A. Day later bought and renamed it the Days Inn, running it as a hotel and restaurant. After a 1933 fire, the building was rebuilt. In 1946, Gordon R. Miller opened a tearoom and soda fountain, and rented a space to Ike Hovey’s barbershop. The building burned again in 1953, and the Assembly of God Church later bought the lot.

	•	A. W. Glass ran a garage beside the bridge ramp from 1914 to 1920. In 1918-19, he and his brother Lawnie set up Hartland’s first electric light plant, bringing electricity to the area for the first time. The photo shows Mr. Glass with his nephew, Graem Simms, son of Ronnie and Laura Glass Simms (Mr. Glass’s sister). Observer 1918, “Last week the Glass Bros. installed their new engine and dynamo, and the juice was turned on Thursday evening. R J MacLean, of Woodstock has been here wiring stores and residences and in a few days at least a portion of the village will be electrically lighted”.

	•	This photo was taken at the river shore at the end of Maple Street, where an ice bridge once allowed travel to the west side. Small fir trees lined the passage, guiding travellers. The photograph predates the construction of the Hartland Bridge in 1901.
	•	Keith & Plummer was owned by Webster Keith and Amasa Plummer, who became business partners after working in the school system; Keith as a teacher and Plummer as a principal. Together, they opened a general store offering furniture, groceries, clothing, china, and housewares, as well as bulk products such as flour, pickles, molasses, and nails. The store also featured furrier and millinery departments. Behind the building stood a cheese factory. They built an additional building to rent to various businesses, including Baird’s fruit stand, Simm’s tinsmith shop, and a confectionery shop.
	•	This view shows Hartland's Main Street looking north before the 1907 fire, which destroyed all the buildings on the left up to the ninth building and part of the tenth.
	•	This view looks down Station Street (later King, known today as Orser Street) toward the Keith & Plummer Store. On the left are David Morgan’s meat shop, the Gillin building, and the C. Humphrey Taylor Block with its colored glass cupola, followed by Keith & Plummer. On the right are a shoe repair shop and residence, as well as John T. G. Carr’s home on Main Street, with his store directly across from his house. A northbound train is just leaving the station.
	•	Maple Street was once Hartland’s Main Street, connecting with High Street at the crest of the hill. From there, it descended through Burtt’s land, known as Burtt’s Hill, and passed the present-day location of the Legion. After crossing the old wooden Becaguimic Bridge, a sharp left turn led up to the north end of High Street. The current Main Street did not receive its name until 1860. Maple Street extended to the river, where the town’s water supply originated from a spring on the riverbank. On the right, the third house was first owned by Dr Curtis and later by Dr Chestnut.

	•	Starting from the left is C. H. Perkins Monuments; many of the monuments made here are found in the Orser and Greenwood cemeteries. Next came McLaughlin’s Massey-Harris store, a supplier of farm machinery. The following building housed the local newspaper, the Chat. Advertising in the Chat costs five cents per line, with an average of seven words per line. Adjoining the Chat was the post office. Residences were located above the street-level shops. Behind these buildings stood warehouses and a gasoline storage building along a rough gravel road that ran along the rear of most west-side businesses and was known to locals as Water Street. 

	•	This was Charles Humphrey Taylor's store before the iconic sign was mounted. The view was from the north, showing his line of goods. At one time, the store operated as Shaw & Dibblee.

	•	This photograph, taken in the early 1900s, shows Burtt’s Hill (now Orser Street) looking up the hill, with the post office and Town Hall on the left today. The man in the light suit was Julius Myers, known as Jack the German. Standing beside the sheep wagon was Clarence (Cad) Hayward, with his son Sandy in front. Riding in the wagon are Mr. and Mrs. Sam Cook. The Scotch Collie dog, Old Fan, was guiding the sheep as they headed toward the train station’s holding pins.
