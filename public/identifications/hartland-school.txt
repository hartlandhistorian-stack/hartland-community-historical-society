	•	James H. Grey became the first teacher at Hartland after 1871. On November 1, 1885, the school earned Superior status. Although this picture is not of the first school in the area, the Hartland Superior School was a modern school for its time, constructed in 1899 for $4,000 and featuring four rooms. Upstairs, the south side housed high school grades eight to eleven, while the north side contained a gramophone and equipment for scientific experiments. A fire exit was not installed until 1918. Both floors had wide halls, and a sink with a water tap was located at the foot of the south stairs on the main floor. Downstairs, the two rooms off the main hall included Grades 1–3 on the left and Grades 4–7 on the right (south). A tool room for garden equipment, wheelbarrows, and bicycles occupied the southwest corner. As the student population grew, the school expanded: in 1921–22, the science lab was relocated to the attic, and both the former lab and tool room were converted into classrooms. The gymnasium was also situated in the attic. There were no indoor toilets; the girls’ outhouse was on the southeast side, and the boys’ was on the northeast corner. The school was located on the east side of Main Street, where Tracey Demerchant's home now stands in 2026.

	•	In the spring of 1921, the school garden on the grounds of Hartland Superior School, also known as McMullin Flat, was assigned to students, who were given seeds and the use of the school’s tools to cultivate, plant, and harvest their own plots. On September 26, 1921, the first school fair was held at Sipprelle’s Garage (across from the school garden plots). Other schools, such as Somerville, Waterville, Coldstream, and Brighten, joined Hartland to exhibit the results of their school gardens, home gardens, pig and poultry clubs, home efficiency projects, and domestic science departments. Judges in each department awarded prizes to those who excelled in their school exhibits.

	•	Students soon outgrew the Hartland Superior School, and it became evident that a new building was needed. On August 27, 1924, a new two-story brick school with a large basement was opened for teachers, school board officials, and pupils, who gathered for an assembly to unofficially mark the occasion. The building featured seven classrooms, an assembly room with a platform, a library, two offices, a cloakroom, modern lavatory and toilet rooms, a furnace room with a fireproof door, an electric bell system, drinking fountains on each floor, and blinds on every window. The official opening was held on September 26, 1924, at 7:30 p.m., with Lieutenant Governor W.F. Todd formally declaring the school open. The building could accommodate up to 400 students, though enrollment at the time was 230.

	•	The new Hartland War Memorial, a 20-foot marble monument, was placed on the school grounds in July 1933 by the Hartland Branch of the Canadian British Empire Service League. Engraved on the front are the names of local heroes who made the supreme sacrifice during World War. The unveiling ceremony, shown in this photo, took place at 2:30 p.m. on Armistice Day, November 11, 1933. One of two mothers whose sons fell in WWI performed the unveiling. Veterans stood in military formation by the memorial as the Hartland Band played appropriate music. Many returned soldiers from Hartland and nearby communities attended the event.

	•	The female cadets of Hartland High School are shown in formation on the west side of the school. Although the specific occasion is unknown, students from surrounding communities often gathered for inter-platoon activities and competitions. The cadets learned discipline, engaged in physical training for strength, practiced presenting arms, saluting, and marching. This photograph was taken in the early 1940s.

	•	Like the female cadets, the male cadets at Hartland High School met every Friday afternoon to train for World War II. They were issued painted wooden guns for practice—crude replicas with brown butts, silver triggers and barrels, and sometimes nails for triggers or a four-inch strip of tin as the trigger guard. Training began with exercises in the basement, followed by outdoor marching in rows to a steady beat. In this picture, the senior cadets are recognizable by their striped pants; some also trained with real guns, though never during school hours. Theoretical instruction was provided using large painted or printed boards and pictures stored in the furnace room. (Details by Harold Plummer)

	•	At Hartland High School, incoming Grade 9 students were required to participate in a week-long initiation; no exceptions. For years, new students could be seen around town dressed up in makeshift costumes. Photographs document this tradition from 1947 to 1955. The initiates had to follow the orders of older students and always let them go first. The activities were orderly and meant to be fun, ensuring that no one took offense. Each day brought a new challenge; one day, you might have to measure the bridge with straight pins while crawling on your hands and knees, or scrub the floor with a toothbrush. This picture, taken in 1952, shows (from left to right): Cheryl McMullin, Betty Stephenson, Rosemarie Carey, Evangeline Sherwood (looking over her shoulder), Eileen Clark, Laura Crabbe, and an unknown girl at the end.
	•	Elmer H. Wiley returned to Hartland in 1948, originally from St. Andrews, to assume the principal position, having served from 1935 to 1937 and then taking several years off. During the war, he worked on the administrative and training staff of the 2nd Battalion, Carleton & York Regiment, and later taught at McAdam. His wife, formerly Inez Smith of Hartland, and their two daughters joined him. Mr. Wiley is set to teach Grade 11.

	•	Hartland High School unveiled its new $100,000 addition on the right side of the building, first opened to the public during the graduation exercises on Thursday night in 1959. This expansion included a large auditorium and six new classrooms. In 1958, eight additional classrooms were created in the older section by removing the old auditorium and using the basement space.

	•	Entering grade four with our teacher, Hazel Nicholson, and music teacher, Myrtle York, meant joining the Rhythm Band. Each student wore a red cape and a top hat held on with elastic, and everyone was given an instrument to play. We practiced on the stage during music class until Mrs. York felt we were ready to perform at assemblies and the music festival. This is the class of 1962.

	•	In 1964, a mix-up during the period when some communities switched to Daylight Saving Time while others remained on Standard Time led Hartland High School students to stage a walkout. The accompanying photo shows several protesting students holding signs that read, "We Want One Time."

	•	After the new gym was built in 1959, it quickly became a hub of activity. Early morning practices at 7 a.m., after-school sessions, and special events such as semifinals, tournaments, and track-and-field meets kept the space in constant use. During this era, sports followed strictly seasonal schedules set by the provincial school board, guaranteeing a defined beginning and end for each activity. Competitions were arranged with schools of similar student populations, fostering fair and spirited rivalries. The community’s enthusiasm for events, whether sports games, concerts, variety shows, music festivals, banquets, or graduations, was remarkable, often filling the gym to standing-room-only capacity. Before the new gym was built, the assembly room on the top floor of the original building served as the main gathering place. Additionally, the Legion hall was equipped as a gymnasium, and its higher ceiling made it an alternative venue for basketball games. 

	•	On March 10, 1949, Hon. J. W. Brittain, the provincial minister of education, announced that New Brunswick public schools would introduce a Grade 12 course beginning in the 1951–52 school year. With this Board of Education decision, the time required to complete junior matriculation was extended from 11 to 12 years. One can only imagine the surprise and disappointment of Grade 11 students who suddenly discovered they would be required to complete an additional year before graduation.
Graduation was always a significant milestone, marked by enthusiastic celebrations. For example, in 1929, the stage was adorned in mauve and gold, the school colours at the time, and students entered the assembly to a march, forming a semi-circle onstage. Ceremonies were rich with tradition, featuring an invocation, a “Who’s Who,” class histories packed with humorous anecdotes, an address offering life advice, a Class Prophecy, class gifts, a Last Will and Testament, and a Valedictory address, all culminating in the awarding of prizes. Although some traditions and school colours have changed over the years, the epic graduation ceremonies, whether held on the school stage or in the local theatre, remain a cherished highlight of school life.

	•	The school balcony overlooking the large modern gymnasium was the go-to spot for watching the action below during games, assemblies, and intramurals. It was more than just a vantage point; it was a favourite hangout for groups of friends or even a place to meet a special someone. Then, one year, we returned to find the balcony completely sealed off. No longer could we gaze over the four-foot wall. For many, its closure marked the end of a cherished chapter and left behind fond memories.

	•	In January 1972, a proposal was made to close Hartland High School and merge it with a new school complex near Woodstock, along with Woodstock High School. The town council strongly opposed the transfer of academic students from Hartland High School. On February 23, 1979, the council approved plans for an addition to Hartland High School. The new annex featured classrooms, a band room, a cafeteria, and offices. The official opening took place on March 21, 1981, and was attended by the Honourable Richard Hatfield. 

	•	The new annex, built in 1959, included a state-of-the-art laboratory complete with tools, slides, instruments, Bunsen burners, pipettes, and microscopes. Everything needed to conduct experiments, take measurements, and gather data in a laboratory setting.

	•	In 1994, students attended D-Day celebrations on June 4 instead of the actual date, June 6.

	•	Before the doors closed for the final time on February 24, 2007, a last, bittersweet picture was taken of the gym—a place that had witnessed so many chapters of our lives. Though the lights are dim and the doors are shut, the gym will never truly sleep. Its walls are alive with memories, quietly listening to the echoes of the past. If you listen closely, you can almost hear the cheers that once rattled the rafters, the squeak of sneakers racing across the floor, and the breathless excitement of games won and lost. You can feel the nervous tension before a big performance, the laughter shared between friends, and the playful banter before each contest. Every corner holds the memory of hard-fought victories, comforting hugs after tough losses, and the unbreakable bonds forged through teamwork.
Even now, the old gym remembers it all—the thump of a basketball, the band's music, the stomp of feet on the bleachers, the clang of water bottles, the nervous giggles and anxious whispers. Though time moves on, the spirit of those moments lingers, making the gym a silent guardian of dreams, friendships, and memories that will never fade.

	•	On February 24, 2007, the last bus left the school grounds as teachers, custodians, and bus drivers gathered to wave farewell at Hartland High School, marking the end of an era in education there.

	•	For the Town of Hartland’s 100th anniversary since incorporation, one of the most memorable festivities was its first-ever all-school reunion, welcoming teachers, students, and graduates of every generation. While no alumni from the original Hartland Superior School remained, hundreds returned from Hartland High and many more from the newer Hartland Community School, which hosted the event. Despite working with a shoestring budget, the organizing team spread the word through Facebook, flyers, and videos, reaching out to class presidents and key contacts from as far back as possible. On Saturday, August 4, 2018, more than 1,000 former students registered at the door. They searched for classmates in designated rooms, enjoyed entertainment in the cafetorium, including a fashion show and live music, and marvelled at memorabilia that filled the gym. Transportation was provided, photos were taken, and a kids’ zone was set up for families. The event surpassed all expectations and became a true celebration of community spirit and shared memories.
