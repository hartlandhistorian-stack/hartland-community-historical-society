	•	Two entrepreneurs, Webster Keith and Amasa Plummer, joined forces in 1894 to construct an attractive two-storey building on a lot south of the present library. This building was destroyed in the fire of 1907. After the site was cleared, they rebuilt: this time, a solid brick two-storey structure with a firewall. The new building housed a millinery, a general grocery offering bulk products, grains, clothing, furniture, housewares, and linens, and operated on a barter system. There was enough space on the street level for another business, while the upper floor accommodated tenants, including a dental parlor, law office, and families. A balcony was added later.

	•	In April 1946, Washington Craig received a building permit for a machine shop. By June of that year, he and his son, Woodford Craig, opened a service station. In 1947, the Craigs were recognized for their generosity when they welcomed hundreds of children to their service station, where Santa Claus greeted them beside a Christmas tree. Each child received a well-filled sack and a special gift. Pastors from various churches led a Christmas service, and Lorne Britton directed the carolling. Residents contributed to a Christmas fund, and after expenses, the remaining funds were donated to the NB Orphanage in Saint John. By 1949, the Craigs had expanded their business to include the sale of cars and farm machinery. In 1952, they began selling v-ploughs and two-wing snowploughs, eventually securing a government contract.

	•	In November 1921, an association was formed to operate an open skating rink and a baseball diamond in the summer. William Carr was the manager. The surface was 75 x 175 feet. Carnivals, concerts, and various fundraising activities were held in the 1930s to keep the rink in operation. Hockey teams were formed, ice carnivals were added, and even music was included in 1936, with a phonograph and speakers. In 1944, a building was ready for both male and female skaters, the rink walls erected, lights put up, and the ground covered with a layer of sawdust, waiting for freezing weather. The picture shows the rink on the McMullin flat, lower Main Street, with an 80 x 200 feet skating surface, the heated rink house, the ice surface encased in wood walls with the end open for the snow pile after shovelling snow off the ice surface. Various people were hired to manage, and the town's cooperation continued to keep it open. Johnny Sproul was hired in 1955. (Photo courtesy of Don Oliver, early 1950s.)

	•	Roy Leonard Stevens, born in 1896, attended Somerville School. At age 17, he began working as a barber in Houlton, Maine. On November 20, 1914, Stevens enlisted in the Canadian Overseas Expeditionary Force and was assigned to the New Brunswick 26th Battalion, which became part of the 5th Infantry Brigade of the Canadian 2nd Division. He served in France and on the Flanders Front from 1915 until the end of the war. In 1919, he married Tessa Aiton and returned to barbering, working first in Florenceville before settling in Hartland for the rest of his career. In the accompanying photo, his barber chair is set up in the front window of David Howell’s pool hall on upper Main Street, where he is shown cutting Jim Hovey’s hair in 1962.

	•	In September 1963, it was announced that Ken Nicholson had purchased the property of H. E. Adams, a home considered one of Hartland’s oldest buildings, which was demolished to make way for a Red & White grocery store affiliated with Atlantic Wholesalers. On October 18, 1969, Ellis and Shaw dissolved their partnership; Gerald Shaw opened the Handy Andy Store, while Leighton Ellis established a Save Easy in late October, which had formerly been the IGA on Main Street. Ellis managed the Save Easy grocery store until 1975. Lona Pelkey then took over the Red & White grocery store, which was destroyed by fire in 1981.

	•	Sheldon and Darlene Hallett wanted to create something meaningful for themselves and their teenagers, so they opened an RV trailer park and gift shop called Bridgeview Campground Gift and Handcraft Centre. After securing permits and meeting the requirement of ten electrical sites, they prepared a total of thirty sites, including a dumping station for dry camping, a popular choice at the time, and set up the gift shop. “We were naïve at first, thinking the gift shop should be stocked with camping gear, but soon realized that visitors wanted items made in New Brunswick. I eventually filled the shelves with local crafts, especially from artisans at the Mactaquac craft fair. Ice was another constant need. Most nights, we wouldn’t get home until 11 p.m., after preparing countless trays of ice for the next day, until we could finally afford an ice machine. People came from everywhere, some flying into Montreal from as far away as Germany. It always amazed us where our visitors came from. We opened on Victoria Day and closed at Thanksgiving, eventually selling to Lona Pelkey in 1978. Just thinking about those days makes me happy,” smiled Darlene Hallett. (Photo courtesy of Darlene Hallett)

	•	In 1919, G. Frank Seeley of Victoria Corner purchased Tom Hurley’s property, located opposite the service station beside the bridge in Hartland. Around 1921, he acquired that service station owned by the Glass Brothers garage and electric power plant, becoming the manager of Hartland Electric Company Ltd. Frank supplied electricity to Hartland, performed electrical wiring, and used a gas engine to generate power. During the Depression years of the 1930s, he operated a second-hand shop in his building, selling bankrupt stock and a variety of old-fashioned goods at remarkably low prices, many of which were still of excellent quality and quite serviceable. In another section of the building, he offered an indoor golf game that was widely patronized. Frank was also an outfitter and, as early as 1932, managed tourist cabins at the top of the bridge hill in Somerville. According to a news clipping, he and his wife, Sadie Clowes, moved to Somerville in 1936 to operate seasonal tourist cabins. Tragically, his home and several cabins were destroyed by fire in 1941.

	•	Hartland’s theatre history dates back to 1914, when silent movies were shown at the Lyric Theatre in Forester Hall (Lower Main, building beside United Church). By 1918, the Starr Theatre operated in the same hall, continuing the tradition of silent films, now accompanied by pianists. In 1922, the Neighborhood Theatre opened for the winter in Burtts Hall on upper Main Street, offering audiences fine talking pictures. After a fire, Wendel Tedlie sold the partially burned building to a group from Saint John, who remodelled and reopened it as the Capitol Theatre. During the war, the government presented films supporting the war effort, including footage of the Carleton York Regiment receiving its colours from His Majesty the King, a ceremony in which Lt. J. Arthur Thornton, son of Mrs. Dwight Ebbett, accepted them. In November 1947, plans were announced for a new, modern Capitol Theatre to be constructed south of the Post Office (now the library), at a cost of $100,000 and managed by Hugh Best, followed by Frank McMullin and John Gillin. The accompanying photo shows the new theatre, which opened on May 21, 1948, with its first movie: “The Fabulous Texan.” Glen Cummings purchased and remodelled it in 1969. The theatre building was torn down in 1986.

	•	The Hartland Clothing Company was founded in 1915 by Anthony Baker, Arthur Dickinson, and Rex York. In 1920, they built a two-storey concrete building with a white front and a basement, which they rented to William Carr for painting cars and carriages. In 1930, the company began selling women’s clothing, hired a female sales associate, and used a truck to deliver goods door-to-door. Fred Dickinson, Arthur’s son, joined in 1935. Baker sold his share to Dickinson and York in 1938. Wilmot Dickinson (Another son of Arthur) joined after World War II. The store was expanded and updated in the 1940s. After Arthur Dickinson died in 1954 and Rex York died in 1958, Fred Dickinson became president. In 1966, the store got a new front and started selling furniture upstairs. Wilmot died in 1977, just before the store burned down in 1980.

	•	Boyer’s summer house was moved to the sidewalk and, over the years, was rented or sold to various businesses, including a jewellery store, Catelli’s Restaurant, a harness shop, and finally Ike Hovey’s Barbershop. The basement was also rented out and once housed a women’s clothing store called the Clothesline. The man on the step was Adrian Hovey, a barber and Ike’s son, before the building was torn down in 1984.

	•	In January 1945, Gordon R. Miller purchased the Days Inn from Gurston Day for $6,000 upon Day’s retirement. Day had operated a hotel, dining room, and fruit and ice cream shop just north of the Bank of Montreal. Miller previously spent 15 years working at V.W. Simm’s store on Main Street. In January 1946, Miller remodelled his new store by installing new windows. On August 9, 1956, a major fire broke out at Keater’s Lunch Bar, with firefighters from Woodstock, Bristol, East Florenceville, and the NB Forest Service joining local crews in an attempt to save the downtown block. The building was not rebuilt after the fire.

	•	The Farmers Telephone Company began in 1905 in Windsor, Carleton County, to provide local farmers with telephone service. The first line linked ten phones from Windsor to Hartland. In 1907, NB Telephone and Central Telephone Company merged, but Farmers Telephone did not join because of high fees. Instead, they expanded quickly, opening exchanges in Hartland, East Florenceville, and Woodstock, to connect them. George O. Britton managed the company, which had about 800 customers by 1919. In 1921, NB Telephone Company bought Farmers Telephone, and George Britton stayed as manager until 1933. The photo shows the new NB Telephone Company office, across from the old Farmers Exchange office, in the Anna Martin building, which is still present today on the east side of Upper Main.

	•	Graydon E. Nevers began working at Aiton Drug Company after graduating from high school in the fall of 1946. He became a pharmacist in 1955 and returned to Aiton Drug Company. On November 11, 1967, Graydon purchased the pharmacy portion of the store, keeping the wholesale business separate. That same month, he moved the drug store into the original post office building across the street and renamed it Nevers Pharmacy Rexall Drug Store. The drug store operated there until 1986 (when it was owned by his son, Glen), when the business relocated to the Hartland Courtyard Mall.

	•	Lloyd Nicholson, just discharged from the RCAF, purchased a building in 1946 that had been formally used as a dwelling and converted it into a hardware store. The building was once the home of John T. G. Carr, who moved the building back from Main Street, past the railroad tracks, to its current location. It was moved to make way for Carr’s general store, which is currently located where Day & Ross is. Lloyd and his wife, Hazel Ellis, lived over the hardware store until they built their home on the corner of Orser and School Street in 1954. (Photo courtesy of Terry Nicholson Logan)

	•	The Blue Jay, with its classic soda fountain and lunch service, was a local spot that evoked childhood memories. Stepping inside transported you back to a carefree day, whether you were dropping in for a treat after skating or enjoying a special outing with friends. It wasn’t just about the food or ice cream floats; it was about reliving happy memories. The Blue Jay was owned by Bob Montgomery in 1967 and later by Brian Hatfield in 1976 before being torn down in 1982.

	•	Valley Equipment Limited originally operated out of the J. Clark and Son building on Main Street before relocating to a new facility in the Industrial Park on January 20, 1966. The new metal building, valued at $50,000, arrived in pre-painted sections and was assembled to measure 100 by 50 feet. On January 1, 1970, Raymond Cook became manager, with an option to buy into the business if it proved profitable. He acquired a 5% stake over the first two years, then purchased 40% in 1975, and by 1978, he owned 100%. In the accompanying photo, Joe Palmer, Louis Robichaud, and Ralph Bard (in the white shirt) are identified, while the others remain unnamed.

	•	At 7:30 a.m. in 1912, a fire broke out in the Commercial Hotel after proprietor George MacCallum built a fire in the kitchen stove and returned to bed. He awoke to the smell of smoke. Located on the east side of the railway track, the building operated as both a hotel and a restaurant. The fire destroyed the CPR dining room on the first floor, ending the tradition of passengers dining in Hartland during train stops; the trade shifted to Woodstock. Although the building was not completely destroyed, the hotel business ended. It was later renovated, with the top floor converted to rental units and at street level adapted into warehouses with loading wharves on the railroad side to easily load farm produce into boxcars.
