	•	Charles Humphrey Taylor, a merchant established in 1891, purchased land at the corner of Main Street and Station Street (later King Street, now Orser Street). He constructed a three-storey brick building crowned with a colored glass cupola and topped by a flagstaff. In December 1895, he moved into the building with his wife and child, occupying the top-floor apartment. Taylor was a member of the farmers' exchange, working to bring healthy, affordable food to his customers.

	•	John Thomas G. Carr, originally from England, arrived in Hartland in the mid to late 1800s. After apprenticing with a local retailer, he opened his own small store in 1884. In 1905, Carr further expanded his enterprise by constructing a modern three-storey brick commercial building, a symbol of solidity, purpose, and stability, which he named The Hartland Department Store. To make way for this impressive structure in the heart of the town’s business district, Carr moved his two-storey residence back across the rail tracks. The relocated house later served as the first home of the Dr. Walter Chestnut Library, located beside Craig’s Manufacturing on Orser Street.
	•	Frank Hagerman arrived in the village that would later become Hartland. He operated a ferry across the river using a set pole and worked for Rev. Benjamin Jewett at the Becaguimic grist mill. Together with his son in 1910, Frank constructed a three-storey concrete block building with a full basement, located across from the bridge ramp. Tenants lived above the retail space, which was elevated above street level and accessible by stairs. Hagerman became well known for his thousands of catchy slogans, most famously, “B 4 U buy C Frank.” A skilled salesman, he sold machinery, soap, and brush products, and also owned a grist mill.
	•	In 1904, John MacLauchlan Co. Ltd. of Woodstock opened a Hartland branch specializing in men’s and boys’ clothing, with A. G. Baker as manager. The business originally operated out of the Burtt Building on upper Main Street (later known as Stedmans and Alumico). In 1908, after a new brick building was constructed, the store relocated to its new home, as seen in this picture. Arthur Dickinson joined Baker, and together they eventually bought out MacLauchlan. With Rex York later joining the partnership, they established the Hartland Clothing Company. In 1920, they built a new white-faced building beside the brick building, and later the Farmer Co-op Store on Main Street.

	•	This two-storey Romanesque building was built in 1912 by the Federal Government to serve as a post office, jail, and armoury. It is the most distinctive structure on the west side of Main Street, Hartland, overlooking the scenic Saint John River Valley. The building became a local historic landmark, playing important roles as a military training centre during both World Wars and as a community gathering place. The second-floor assembly room was managed by the Militia Department. The clock was added in 1913. Today, the building houses the Dr. Walter Chestnut Library.

	•	Hartland’s first bank, the Peoples Bank of Halifax, opened in 1897 in a building that rounded the corner on Orser Street, constructed by John T. G. Carr. The Bank of Montreal acquired it in 1905. In the winter of 1920, Henry Post was hired to build a new neoclassical brick building on Main Street. On July 18, 1921, the Bank of Montreal moved its local offices into this impressive new location on Main Street.
	•	Fred Stevens (1873–1924) received his first printing press at age 13. In 1897, he began publishing “The Advertiser” as Hartland’s first weekly newspaper; previously, it had been a monthly. Ill health forced Stevens to stop in 1904, and George MacLauchlan of Woodstock temporarily filled the gap with his paper, “The Chat,” until his building was lost in the 1907 fire. MacLauchlan gave up publishing the Chat in 1909. On June 4, 1909, Stevens launched “The Observer” from a cement block building on Orser Street, just past the railroad tracks. In 1920, he moved the operation to a Main Street building across from the bridge ramp. That building was demolished in 2001.
	•	The Hartland outdoor ice rink was located at the bottom of Carr’s Hill, behind a warehouse once owned by C. Rideout and later by William MacLeod. Pictured on the left is Ethel Christian Bailey, and third from the left is Dorothy Keith MacLeod. The identities of the other individuals are unknown.
	•	Pictured was the roller rink on the third floor of the original Foresters’ Hall. Built in 1897 by the Hartland Independent Order of Foresters, the hall was a simple two-storey, square clapboard building featuring a wide stairwell, narrow wood panelling throughout, and a barrel-vaulted ceiling. The second floor hosted their meetings, while the first floor was rented for community events. By 1910, a roller rink operated on the first floor. In 1912, silent movies were shown upstairs, accompanied by a live pianist. In 1919, Fred Smith purchased the building, turning the first floor into a garage and the second into a paint shop. In 1922, the Star Theatre opened on the second floor. Smith then added a third floor and partial basement, and by 1922–23, a roller rink operated on the new third floor, with a Wurlitzer electric organ replacing the old hurdy-gurdy for music. The roller rink remained in operation until the 1940s. The building is located beside the United Church on the east side of lower Main Street.
	•	In February 1944, work began on a new concrete eastern approach as part of a major bridge upgrade. The project included installing concrete piers to support a wider deck and adding a covered pedestrian walkway along the south side of the bridge. The Department of Transportation completed the work, using local wood to refloor the bridge. The photo captures the completed improvements.
	•	In the 1930s, Frank Camp ran a meat market on the street level of what was once the Commercial Hotel, a large building beside the railroad tracks, with warehouses behind the storefront. Gerald Shaw worked part-time at Camp’s Market. After returning from the war, Shaw and his friend, Leighton Ellis, bought the store and renamed it 'Ellis and Shaw,' expanding the business to include groceries as well as meat. They operated the store until 1966. This photo was taken in 1952. (Courtesy of Necedah Shaw Fairgrieves)

	•	A large home once stood on Main Street, owned by Mr. and Mrs. George Boyer. The front room served as a drug store, while the back housed the post office. At the rear of their property was a shed where Hector McLean lived, doing handywork, sharpening axes and saws, and repairing tools. Later, the shed was moved closer to the street, renovated into a summer house, and then moved again to the edge of Main Street. Over the years, several businesses operated from this building; the last was Adrian Hovey’s Barbershop, which was demolished in 1984.

	•	Charles E. Allen, a barber, is pictured taking a break in front of his shop on upper Main Street (Building still present). After arriving in Hartland in 1893, he quickly established his barbershop and became a mentor to many, including Ike Hovey and Roy Stevens, who later opened their own shops. In those early days, a haircut cost 15 cents and a shave 10 cents. Outside of work, Allen was passionate about horse racing. He travelled to various events with his chestnut stallion, Calumet Duds, and sold the horse in 1936 when it was six years old. On Sundays, Allen would take out his dappled horse, driving a carriage with his daughter, Edna and his wife seated in the back, while he sat up front in his suit, tie, and felt hat. His wife managed the first Farmers Telephone office above his barber shop.

	•	This building was originally constructed in 1905 by John T. G. Carr as The Hartland Store, a general merchandise business. In August 1911, Samuel Miller, manager of Estey & Curtis, purchased the property, relocating his pharmacy into the spacious three-storey brick building and expanding into retail. The business was renamed Estey and Curtis Wholesale & Retail Druggist. At the time, Frank Aiton worked as a pharmacist. When Mr. Miller expanded his growing business to Fredericton, Mr. Aiton took over as manager of the Hartland Store and, in 1946, purchased it. The business was then renamed Aiton Drug Co. Ltd. Wholesale & Retail Druggists. Current headquarters of Day & Ross Transportation Group.

	•	The historic McMullin Homestead on lower Main Street was lost to fire on February 11, 1933, leaving three families without a home. Only two chimneys, each with seven fireplaces once used for cooking, survived the blaze. Built in 1830 by Rev. Charles McMullin, who married Lawrence Wilsey’s daughter, the homestead was one of the area’s oldest and most storied buildings. Over the years, it served as a hotel, tavern, and steamer stop for travellers on the Saint John River. The fire was discovered by W. P. McMullin, who quickly alerted his family, but despite help from neighbors and the fire department, the house could not be saved. Residents, Mr. and Mrs. W. P. McMullin, Mr. and Mrs. Claude McMullin, and Mr. and Mrs. Milo McMullin, lost both their home and many treasured family heirlooms. Known for its solid construction and long history in the McMullin family, the homestead’s loss marked the end of an era for this region of New Brunswick.

	•	Henry Bradley once owned the building on upper Main Street, running a shoe shop where he made and repaired footwear. In 1934, the Rigby family bought the property and operated a restaurant before selling it to Goldie Miller, who continued the restaurant and added a confectionery business. Pictured in the doorway is Mrs. Roy Rigby, mother of Carl Rigby.

	•	Thomas Simms, once a blacksmith, rented space from Keith and Plummer on the west side of the street. His wife, Mary, ran a bake oven, selling baked goods, soft drinks, and sweets. In 1901, Thomas built a new store beside the Taylor Block, naming it Fruits and Fancies. The wooden building had a shop at street level and living quarters upstairs, reached by an outside stairway. After Thomas’s death in 1918, their son Vernon returned home to help Mary. Vernon grew the business by making speciality ice cream, using the old-school on-the-flat method for production after 1924. General Dairies later bought his ice cream business, and Vernon moved to Woodstock. The store changed hands several times: James Miller bought it in 1955 and called it Millers; Bob Montgomery purchased it in 1967, renaming it The Blue Jay; and Brian Hatfield acquired it in 1976. The building was demolished in 1982.

	•	Behind the C. Humphrey Taylor block on Orser Street stood a small building with two retail shops. The left side once housed the town's first lending library, a meat shop, and a jewellery store, while the right side was a barber shop run by Harold and Weldon Bradley. Before its demolition in 1982, the building was home to the Carleton Craft Gift Shop and Tourist Information Centre.

	•	This later photo shows Simm’s ice-cream parlour on Main Street, with Vernon Simms standing in the doorway. Vernon developed a rich, velvety ice cream that became so popular he needed to rent extra space to keep up with demand. He sold his ice cream throughout the river valley. General Dairies eventually purchased the business, giving Vernon shares and appointing him manager in Woodstock. Vernon kept the store until 1955, when he sold it to James Miller. It later changed hands to Bob Montgomery and then Brian Hatfield. The building was demolished in 1982.
