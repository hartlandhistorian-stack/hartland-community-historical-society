	•	In 1968, Alumico Building Supply, operated by Brian and Russell Smith, was destroyed by fire. This was one of Hartland’s oldest buildings, originally constructed by George R. Burtt well before 1900, famously built over a tree stump. Burtt initially operated a general store there, with a meeting hall that served the Anglican Church until their new church was built. In 1887, David Keswick took over the store and later sold it to Wendel Tedlie, who opened the Neighbourhood Theatre. After a fire partially damaged the theatre, Tedlie chose not to rebuild. Instead, he sold the property to a group from Saint John in 1938, who remodelled it and opened the modern Capitol Theatre. The Capitol Theatre group eventually relocated further down the street, constructing a new building beside the Post Office, which opened in 1948. The building was sold to the Stedman 5 & 10 chain, which subsequently moved to the J. Clark and Son building in 1966, and the original building was sold to Russell Smith.
	•	At around 9 a.m. on Saturday, January 20, 1968, while Blaine Hallett and his men were working on the garage floor, an explosion occurred at the Irving station. It is believed that leaking gas may have been ignited by the furnace. Immediately, flames engulfed the building, leaving no time to retrieve account books, cash, or any belongings. The explosion blew out the windows and doors, sending them about fifty feet from the structure. Losses included cash, cheques, credit card receipts, equipment, tires, parts, and tools, totalling more than $15,000. Fortunately, Blaine had one cheque in his pocket, a $1,000 government payment for school bus repairs and servicing.

	•	Hatfield Industries’ head office is on the left, with the Hatfield chip plant on the right. After a devastating fire in 1946 destroyed the dehydration plant, Heber Hatfield sought a new opportunity for the local potato industry. Hartland, surrounded by potato fields and farms, had long relied on supplying dehydrated potatoes for allied forces during two wars, making local farmers dependent on Hatfield for a market. During a trip south, Hatfield discovered potato chips and envisioned a new future for Hartland—potato chip production. “Golden Crisp” potato chips, manufactured by Hatfield Industries Ltd., were set to go into production in 1950.

	•	In 1968, F. H. Hatfield, President of Hatfield Industries Limited in Hartland, N.B., and N. Kishfy, Managing Director of Humpty Dumpty Potato Chip Company in Lachine, Quebec, jointly announced that Hatfield Industries Limited had become an affiliate of Humpty Dumpty. Mr. Hatfield subsequently assumed the role of Chairman of the Board. As seen in the photo, the Humpty Dumpty sign is twisted because a fire broke out at around 3:30 a.m. on October 6, 1981, as recounted by employee Dana Clark, who was working that night. “The fryer had been shut down during the day for maintenance, which allowed us to catch up on cleaning and painting. While we were taking our lunch break in the dining room, Dale Hayes walked in and calmly told Stillman Tibbits he’d better come: ‘I think she’s all afire.’ With no cell phones, someone had to drive to the fire hall to push the button to sound the long, continuous blast. Years of accumulated chip grease fueled the blaze, turning the building into a red-hot inferno, and everyone could only watch in disbelief."

	•	Eleven firetrucks and as many as 75 firefighters from here and neighboring communities battled a massive blaze in the commercial section of Main Street for six and a half hours after it was discovered at 4 a.m., August 25, 1980. The fire began behind Stedmans and quickly swept down the street, leaping from roof to roof and tearing through firewalls as if they didn’t exist. Hartland Clothing, Wright and Everett, and the head office of Day & Ross were destroyed, while Never’s Pharmacy suffered extensive water damage, with the total losses estimated at $3 million. Onlookers watched in disbelief as history seemed to repeat itself: in 1907, the same commercial district burned in an eerily similar fashion on the same land. When our ancestors rebuilt with brick and firewalls, it proved no defence against the devastation.

	•	Looking down on an organized chaos of tangled fire hoses well into the day on August 25, 1980, firefighters battled not just flames, but also for the livelihoods of many local citizens.

	•	The New Brunswick Seed Grower Plant on lower Main caught fire on October 16, 1984.

	•	On May 19, 1988, a fire in Hartland made headlines as far away as Bangor. When firefighters sifted through the debris, they discovered a coffin containing the 100-year-old skeleton of a middle-aged man. Nearby, they found robes inscribed with a skull and crossbones, a hangman’s noose, flags, swords, and dishes. Rumors quickly swept through the town, though investigators remained silent. Was it an unsolved murder or evidence of a secret cult in the quiet farming community? The fire chief reportedly ruled out arson and foul play. As it turned out, the once-active Knights of Pythias had used these items for their secretive initiation ceremonies and were sworn to secrecy. The skeleton was turned over to the RCMP, and the Rideout building was destroyed.

	•	On the evening of May 7, 2005, around 8 p.m., John Craig, owner of Craig Manufacturing, arrived at the 59-year-old plant he and his father had built—only to watch it become engulfed in flames. Quick thinking allowed for the safe recovery of the file server, which contained essential engineering drawings, as well as accounting, administration, and human resources files. Immediate action was taken to get the business running again and support the 85 to 90 staff members. The presence of argon, oxygen, and propane gases, combined with flammable materials used in painting attachments, heightened emergency workers’ fears that an explosion could devastate much of Hartland. Consequently, about 400 people were evacuated. Craig Manufacturing produces snow plows, trailers, land-clearing equipment, specialized buckets, and log-handling machinery.

	•	The fire was discovered around one o’clock on Monday morning on July 15, 1907, in the rear of the W. F. Thornton drug store. Amasa Plummer, who lived next door above the Keith & Plummer store, was awakened by smoke and flames and immediately sounded the alarm. After two rings of the bell and the terrifying midnight cry of “fire.” Those two taps were enough to rouse the neighborhood. Before the firefighters arrived with their apparatus, flames were already soaring skyward behind the drug store. The blaze swept along the west side of Main Street, from the ramp and toll house at the Hartland Bridge to the Tracey building just beyond the current library (which was not affected, as it was built in 1912). Explosions shattered windows on the buildings across the street. In total, twenty-three businesses and twelve family and professional residences were lost.

	•	On March 1, 2024, the Covered Bridge Chip plant in Waterville was destroyed by fire; a total loss. According to CBC, firefighters from several departments responded to the blaze at the Covered Bridge Potato Chips factory near Hartland, a major employer in the New Brunswick community. The call came in at 6 p.m., and 20 to 30 employees were safely evacuated. The business began as a potato distribution plant in 2004 and expanded into potato chip production in 2009. This four-generation family farming enterprise grew more than 500 acres of potatoes for the chip manufacturing industry.

	•	This photograph offers another view of the aftermath of the July 15, 1907 fire, highlighting the destruction of the east side of the Hartland Bridge ramp and toll house. The blaze not only devastated the west side of Main Street’s commercial area in 1907, but the very same area, rebuilt by local businesses, was destroyed again in 1980.

	•	On May 12, 1945, a devastating fire reduced the three-storey brick building owned by the United Farmers Store to a gaunt shell of blackened walls. As one of Hartland’s leading general stores, its loss was deeply felt, with damages estimated at up to $20,000. The blaze also destroyed the offices on the second floor of Fred Boyd, the town magistrate, as well as those of R. D. Stewart and G. R. McCutcheon. The building, owned by a stock company of local farmers led by President William Bubar, had only recently been purchased after years of renting. Frank Plummer managed the store at the time. Thanks to the swift response of the town’s fire brigade, the fire was contained before reinforcements from Bristol and Woodstock arrived. Neighboring businesses, Hartland Clothing Company to the south and Plummer’s Store to the north, suffered only minor water damage, as their stock and ledgers had been removed as a precaution.

	•	This photo shows the massive dehydration plant and starch factory in the 1940s, owned by Heber Hatfield, who secured a WWII contract to produce dehydrated potatoes for Allied forces. Potatoes from local farmers were washed, cut, and placed on trays in a drying tunnel, where intense heat removed their moisture over five hours, reducing them to pieces the size of macaroni. The dehydrated potatoes were sealed in waterproof cans designed to be dropped by airplane to soldiers' camps. Three 75-pound bags of fresh potatoes became a single 15-pound can of dehydrated product. Each can was weighed, labelled, and sealed by soldering. Remarkably, just one can provided 1,000 servings; simply soaking the contents in water before cooking restored them to their original consistency.

	•	At 4 a.m. on November 7, 1946, an alarm sounded when a plant worker discovered a blaze in an especially vulnerable, powder-dry section of the facility. Within minutes, the entire building was engulfed in thick black smoke. Firefighters from neighboring communities arrived with their trucks and hoses, but the starch and dehydration plant was already beyond saving; their focus shifted to protecting nearby homes by keeping them wet. Although the dehydration plant was not operating at the time, all the machinery remained inside, including a new drum dryer and a complex centrifuge intended for a new glucose production venture. The fire began in the old dry houses, which were slated for removal. The building, located beside the CPR train tracks, encompassed 6,000 square feet of floor space. The loss left fifty plant workers, truck drivers, and local potato farmers unemployed. Estimated damages totaled $300,000.

	•	In November 1959, Joe Martin’s hardware store was destroyed by fire after he attempted to defrost frozen pipes with a blow torch, resulting in a $50,000 loss. After the retirement of the late James Montgomery, Mr. Martin had purchased the Montgomery hardware business. Following two devastating fires at his premises, he constructed the Handy-Andy store, which he operated until 1964. The building stood two lots north of the present library.

	•	At approximately 10:30 a.m. on Wednesday, October 16, 1963, a fire broke out in the basement of the United Baptist Church, on the site where the rebuilt Baptist Church stands today, while workers were performing repairs. Upstairs, a Philathea class was quilting when they detected the smell of smoke and sounded the alarm. Firefighters from Hartland responded immediately and were soon joined by crews from Woodstock and Florenceville. The sprawling building, enclosed with a steel roof, made firefighting efforts especially challenging. Thick clouds of smoke billowed from the doors and eaves as flames rapidly climbed the partitions. Another devastating loss for Hartland, a historic church built in 1898.
